Independent Clinical–Computational Research Lab

Core Idea

Explore new computational and mathematical methods for studying clinical data, with the goal of identifying research questions that are scientifically interesting, clinically meaningful, feasible with modest resources, and potentially publishable.

This is not primarily a medical-app project and not a generic “use AI to cure disease” effort. The focus is on improving or testing the methods used to extract reliable knowledge from medical data.

Collaboration Model

Clinical side: medical relevance, patient context, interpretation, feasibility, access to appropriately governed clinical data.

Computational side: statistics, mathematical modelling, simulation, machine learning where justified, causal reasoning, temporal analysis, uncertainty estimation, visualization, and reproducible research pipelines.

Research Direction

Search for problems where existing medical research methods may be limited by:

small or noisy datasets,

irregular longitudinal records,

heterogeneous patient populations,

missing or biased observations,

weak subgroup detection,

poor modelling of disease trajectories,

treatment-response variability,

uncertainty being hidden by aggregate statistics,

methods developed mainly on Western populations and insufficiently tested on Indian patients.

The ideal project should ask not only:

“What does this dataset tell us?”

but also:

“Can we develop or test a better way of learning from this kind of clinical data?”

India-Relevant Opportunity

Where scientifically justified, prioritize questions involving Indian clinical populations, local disease patterns, treatment pathways, demographic diversity, resource constraints, or datasets that are underrepresented in international literature.

The aim is not to force an India angle, but to identify cases where Indian data can reveal limitations in existing assumptions or methods.

What the Exploration Phase Should Produce

A map of relevant medical + computational literature.

A shortlist of promising research questions.

For each idea:

clinical significance,

methodological novelty,

required data,

feasibility,

ethical/privacy considerations,

expected difficulty,

publication potential,

reasons the idea may fail.

A ranked set of 3–5 serious candidate projects.

Simple exploratory charts or conceptual diagrams where useful.

A research journal documenting searches, rejected ideas, assumptions, and decisions.

Research Philosophy

We should actively try to disprove our own ideas.

A negative result, failed hypothesis, or demonstration that a proposed method does not outperform existing approaches can still be valuable if the study is rigorous and the question is worthwhile.

The first project should be small enough to complete, serious enough to publish, and structured so that it can grow into a broader programme of clinical–computational research.
